Five engineers whose fingerprints are all over the last 90 days

Why volume metrics fail at PostHog specifically: of merged PRs were approved by an AI reviewer instead of a full human review (the stamphog label — GitHub's own description: "Request AI approval (no full review)"). And five of the seven highest-volume reviewers in this repo are bots: stamphog alone left 8,460 reviews, more than any human. Counting PRs or reviews here measures automation, not engineering — so this dashboard scores neither.

So impact here is not commits, lines, or PR count — all present in the data, all deliberately worth zero. It is four things a leader actually cares about: how many teammates you unblock, whether your work lands on surfaces others depend on, how much human-reviewed work you actually land, and whether you carry the unglamorous fix/perf load. Each score is a cohort percentile that opens into the named PRs behind it. This is evidence to check, not a verdict to accept — click any engineer and go falsify it.

Methodology — and what it deliberately ignores

Data: every merged pull request in PostHog/posthog over the 90 days , pulled from the GitHub GraphQL API one day at a time (so nothing is lost to the API's 1,000-result search cap), joined to the repo's git history for file-level data. Bot and automation accounts are excluded from every figure.

What stamphog actually is

An earlier draft of this dashboard read the stamphog label as "AI-authored". That was wrong, and it is corrected here. GitHub's own label description is "Request AI approval (no full review)" — the PRs are written by named human engineers; the label means the author asked a bot to approve rather than waiting on a full human review. These PRs are counted normally and simply attract little human review attention, which the Reviewed-throughput pillar already reflects without any special-casing.

Why not commits, lines or PR count?

All three reward volume and punish the senior engineer who spends the morning in review, or who lands 40 lines in the query engine that unblock five teams. They are in the dataset for reference, but contribute zero to the score.

The four pillars

  • Review leverage (35%) — the count of distinct engineers whose PRs you reviewed (60%) and your number of substantive reviews (40%). Substantive means CHANGES_REQUESTED or COMMENTED; a bare approval is a rubber stamp and scores nothing. Distinct people, not review count, so reviewing one teammate 50 times does not look like reviewing 50 teammates.
  • Blast radius (25%) — does your code land where others work? Half is the number of shared-surface files you touched (files + distinct engineers also touched in the window); half is the count of distinct engineers who touch the same files as you. A big diff in an isolated corner scores near zero; a small diff in a hot path scores high.
    The "Shared code they own" panel is display-only and ranks files differently: for each engineer we count how many times they changed each file in the window, divide by the square root of that file's total churn so busy files don't automatically win, and multiply by the log of how many engineers share the file. Files only one person ever touched are excluded, so the panel shows code an engineer repeatedly works on and shares with colleagues, not just the repo's most crowded files.
  • Reviewed throughput (25%) — your merged work weighted by the attention it attracted, not its size. Attention = distinct reviewers + 0.5×inline review threads + 0.25×comments. This uses the org's own scrutiny as the signal of consequence: a 2,000-line PR nobody read scores below a 20-line PR that five engineers argued over.
  • Work mix & load (15%) — share of your PRs that are fixes or performance work (60%) plus the breadth of work types you cover (40%). Credits the person keeping production healthy, which feature-count metrics systematically miss.

What "attention" counts — and what it used to count

Bots author 71% of issue comments and over half of inline review comments in this repo, and AI reviewers comment roughly in proportion to diff size. An earlier version counted raw comment and thread totals, which quietly let lines-of-code back in through the pillar built to replace it. Attention now counts only human reviewers, human inline review comments and human issue comments, all excluding the PR author. That dropped the top-decile threshold from 6.5 to and this pillar's correlation with PR count from 0.98 to 0.86.

A change that was tested and rejected

To blunt the remaining volume sensitivity we tested adding median attention per PR as a density term. It was rejected on the evidence: it takes only 26 distinct values across 154 engineers (so one comment on one PR can swing a score more than the gap between 1st and 3rd), correlates just −0.28 with AI-approval share, and its largest beneficiaries are engineers with two to five PRs. It also penalises requesting AI approval on trivial changes, which is PostHog's own documented triage. Peak-based and reweighting alternatives were tested too and changed almost nothing. The honest conclusion was to fix the input rather than add a statistic, and to state the residual bias plainly above.

Scoring

Each sub-metric is converted to a percentile within this 90-day cohort, so "78" means "higher than 78% of eligible contributors on that dimension" — not an arbitrary point total. Pillars combine 35/25/25/15 into the final score. Click any engineer to see the raw counts and the actual PRs behind every number.

Honest limitations

  • Glue work is invisible. Incident response, the support-hero rotation, design review, RFCs, mentoring and unblocking in Slack produce no PRs. Tanya Reilly's Being Glue describes exactly the contributor this dashboard cannot see.
  • Long-cycle work is penalised. Eight weeks rewriting a query planner lands as one or two PRs and scores near the floor.
  • Greenfield is penalised. Blast radius rewards crowded files, so an engineer alone in a new product area scores low by construction — and a well-factored isolated module looks like low impact when it is good design.
  • Attention rewards contentiousness. A PR with 40 comments may be badly designed rather than important; per-PR attention is capped at to blunt this, but the bias remains.
  • New hires are structurally disadvantaged by percentile scoring over a fixed 90-day window.
  • Research (SPACE, Forsgren et al. 2021; DORA; Deming) is consistent that individual ranking on delivery telemetry is unsound — these frameworks are deliberately scoped to teams and systems. Treat this as a prompt for conversation, not a performance rating.
  • Blast radius uses 90-day co-editing as a proxy for architectural centrality, not real dependency analysis.
  • Scores are relative to this repo and this window, so they compare contributors to each other, not to an absolute bar.
  • Eligibility floor () keeps one-off contributors from distorting the percentile base.
